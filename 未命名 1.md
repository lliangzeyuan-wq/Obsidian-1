As a science writer, the author has discovered that the practice of journaling, also known as expressive writing, does not only soothe a troubled soul, but can also boost people's physical health. This conclusion was supported by a famous experiment conducted in the 1980s, where a series of students were asked to write a short essay for 15 minutes on four consecutive days. Some of the participants were encouraged to write about the most traumatic or upsetting experience of their lives, exploring their deepest thoughts and feelings about the episode, while others were assigned to write about trivial daily things, such as descriptions of their dorm rooms or the shoes they were wearing.
 
Although many students who wrote about their trauma left the lab feeling sadder after confronting their painful emotions, over the following six months, they paid around half as many visits to the student health center as the participants in the control group. The experiment with blood tests and subsequent follow-up studies further revealed the effects of expressive writing: after four consecutive days of writing about their feelings, people's white blood cells started replicating more quickly in response to foreign invaders, which meant their immune system functioned more efficiently; additional studies also found that expressive writing can accelerate recovery after a biopsy, reduce blood pressure and improve lung function, as it calms people down and restores the body's immune defenses that are weakened by long-term emotional distress.
 
To answer why writing can be so cathartic in the first place, one reasonable possibility is that externalizing our inner thoughts and feelings through words gives us more mental space to think about other things, releases our cognitive resources that are occupied by overwhelming emotions, reduces our stress, and can even improve our sleep if we do it before bedtime. Besides its core cathartic function, expressive writing could perform a similar function of clearing our mental workspace: it helps wipe out the sources of negative rumination in our mind, freeing up our working memory so that we can devote more energy to the things that truly matter.
 
Equally importantly, writing our worries down can create a sense of "psychological distance", which allows us to take a more philosophical and rational attitude towards our problems. For instance, if we have been contemplating hurt caused by others, we might find it easier to see things from their viewpoint after writing about the experience, rather than being trapped in intense negative emotions. To maximize the benefits of expressive writing, there are several evidence-based ways to follow: without losing too much spontaneity, we can aim to give our account a clear narrative structure, such as sketching out the background to our emotions and the chain of events that led up to them; we can also be precise about our exact feelings of disappointment or disillusionment, as pinpointing those feelings can help us gain better insight; finally, we can consider how the situation we experienced affirms our good qualities and the things we value.
 
As for the author herself, her personal experience with journaling has evolved significantly over time. Like many anguished teens, she once treated her diary as her best friend: she would write in her diary when she was in a terrible mood, and found that the process of writing could calm her down, as if a physical pressure had been lifted from her chest. As she grew up, she gradually used journaling as a tool to clear her mental workspace and process her negative emotions, finding that after writing in her journal, her anxieties would recede into the background. Now, even though her teenage anger is well behind her, her journal continues to be a source of solace for her, and she uses her knowledge of psychology to take full advantage of it: whenever she feels overwhelmed, a few minutes of writing can make the world feel a little more manageable.
